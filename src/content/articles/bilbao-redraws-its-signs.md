The brief was one line long: make the signs readable again. Behind it were three thousand street signs, four languages of history, and a city council that wanted the work done before the next election.

## Walking every street

The studio started by photographing every sign in the old town. Some were enamel, some were painted, a few were carved into stone. No two used the same letters.

Instead of picking one style, the team drew a new alphabet from the best of what they found. The result keeps the wide, sturdy capitals of the oldest enamel signs and adds a lowercase that reads well from a moving bus.

## Testing at walking speed

Every version went up on a real wall for a week. The team stood across the street with a stopwatch and counted how long it took people to find a name. Letters that slowed people down were redrawn, sometimes four or five times.

## The hard parts

The council wanted a logo on every sign. The studio pushed back, and the compromise was a small mark at the bottom edge that nobody notices unless they look for it. Budget cuts in the second year meant the hand-painted versions were dropped from all but the oldest streets.

## What other cities can take from it

- Survey what you have before you draw anything new.
- Test on real walls, at real distances, with real people walking past.
- Protect the alphabet first. Logos, colours and layouts can change later.

The last sign went up in September. Most people have not noticed, which is exactly what the studio wanted.
